## What is an SMPS?

An SMPS, or switched mode power supply, is a power supply that regulates its output by switching a transistor fully on and fully off at high frequency. The chopped voltage is then filtered by inductors and capacitors into smooth DC.

You use dozens of them every day. Phone chargers, laptop adapters, LED drivers, TV and computer supplies, and the [24 V supplies in industrial panels](https://instrumentationblog.in/24-volts-in-plc-systems/) are almost all switched mode designs.

The two building blocks you may already know, the [buck converter](/power-electronics/buck-converter/) and the [boost converter](/power-electronics/boost-converter/), are themselves simple SMPS circuits. A mains powered SMPS adds rectification, isolation and feedback around the same switching idea.

### 1. EMI filter

A small filter of common mode chokes and X and Y capacitors stops the switching noise from travelling back into the mains wiring. It also protects the supply from fast transients on the line.

### 2. Rectifier and bulk capacitor

A bridge rectifier turns the AC into pulsating DC, and a large electrolytic capacitor smooths it. From 230 V AC the capacitor charges to about 230 × √2 ≈ 325 V DC. Higher power supplies add an active power factor correction stage here, usually a boost converter.

### 3. High frequency switch and transformer

A MOSFET chops the high DC voltage at tens to hundreds of kHz. The transformer steps the pulses down and provides safety isolation between the mains and the output.

### 4. Output rectifier and filter

Fast diodes or synchronous MOSFETs rectify the transformer output, and an LC filter removes the switching ripple.

### 5. Feedback and PWM controller

A precision reference such as a TL431 compares the output with its set point and drives an optocoupler. The optocoupler carries this error signal across the isolation barrier to the PWM controller, which adjusts the duty cycle.

## Why high frequency makes it small

A transformer's core must not saturate. For a given voltage, the number of turns times the core area must rise as frequency falls. That is why a 50 Hz mains transformer is so heavy.

An SMPS runs its transformer at 50 kHz to several hundred kHz, which is a thousand times higher or more. The same power then needs a far smaller core and fewer turns. Filter inductors and capacitors shrink for the same reason.

<Callout type="insight">
Higher frequency shrinks the magnetics but increases switching loss in every cycle. Choosing the switching frequency is always a trade off between size and efficiency.
</Callout>

## Types of SMPS

| Type | Isolated | Typical power | Where it is used |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Buck, boost, buck-boost | No | mW to kW | Point of load regulators, battery devices |
| Flyback | Yes | Up to about 100 to 150 W | Chargers, adapters, auxiliary supplies |
| Forward | Yes | About 100 to 500 W | Industrial and telecom supplies |
| Push pull, half bridge | Yes | Hundreds of W | Inverters, bench supplies |
| Full bridge, LLC resonant | Yes | Hundreds of W to kW | Server, TV and EV charger supplies |

The flyback is the most common offline type because it needs only one switch and the transformer doubles as the energy storage inductor.

## How a flyback SMPS regulates

While the MOSFET is on, current builds up in the primary winding and energy is stored in the transformer's magnetic field. The output diode is reverse biased, so the output capacitor feeds the load.

When the MOSFET turns off, the winding voltages reverse. The diode conducts and the stored energy flows to the output. In continuous conduction the ideal output is:

$$
V_{out} = V_{in}\,\frac{N_s}{N_p}\,\frac{D}{1 - D}
$$

The controller changes D to hold the output steady as the input voltage or load changes. For the step-up and step-down behaviour of the DC-DC stage, the [flyback transformer calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/flyback-transformer/) helps with first estimates of turns ratio and inductance.

## SMPS vs linear power supply

| Feature | Linear supply | SMPS |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Efficiency | Often 30% to 60% | Often 80% to 95% |
| Size and weight | Large 50 Hz transformer, heavy | Small and light |
| Input range | Narrow | Wide, often 85 to 265 V AC |
| Output noise | Very low | Switching ripple and EMI |
| Complexity | Simple | More complex |
| Best use | Audio, precision analog, low power | Almost everything else |

## Advantages and disadvantages of SMPS

The main advantage is efficiency. Because the switch is either fully on or fully off, little power is lost in it, so an SMPS runs cool and needs only a small heat sink. The second advantage is size: high frequency magnetics make a 65 W laptop adapter fit in a pocket. A third is the wide input range, which lets one design work on mains supplies around the world.

The drawbacks come from the same fast switching. Sharp voltage and current edges create electromagnetic interference that must be filtered. The output carries some switching ripple and noise, which can disturb sensitive audio or measurement circuits. The design is also more complex, and a fault in the primary side can expose dangerous voltages.

## Protection features in an SMPS

A good SMPS protects itself and the load. Most controllers include several of these functions:

- **Soft start:** the duty cycle rises slowly at power up, so the output capacitors charge without a large inrush current.
- **Overcurrent and short circuit protection:** a current sense resistor in the MOSFET source limits peak current on every cycle and shuts down or hiccups during a short.
- **Overvoltage protection:** if the feedback loop fails, a separate sensing circuit stops switching before the output rises too high.
- **Thermal shutdown:** the controller stops when its temperature passes a safe limit.
- **Brown out protection:** the supply stays off until the input voltage is high enough to run correctly.

## Working safely on an SMPS

The bulk capacitor in an offline SMPS holds over 300 V DC and can stay charged after the plug is pulled. Always discharge it through a suitable resistor and confirm with a meter before touching the primary side. Use an isolation transformer when probing the primary with an oscilloscope, because the scope ground is tied to earth.

## Key formulas

| Quantity | Formula | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bulk capacitor voltage | Vdc ≈ Vac × √2 | 230 V AC gives about 325 V DC |
| Flyback output (CCM) | Vout = Vin × (Ns / Np) × D / (1 − D) | Ideal |
| Reflected voltage | Vor = (Np / Ns) × (Vout + Vf) | Seen by the MOSFET |
| MOSFET stress | Vds ≈ Vdc(max) + Vor + spike | Choose 600 to 800 V parts |
| Efficiency | η = Pout / Pin | |

## Worked example

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example">

**Given:** a flyback SMPS from 230 V AC to 12 V, turns ratio Np / Ns = 10, diode drop 0.5 V

**Find:** bulk DC voltage, duty cycle and MOSFET voltage stress at 265 V AC maximum input

**Solution:**

1. Vdc = 230 × √2 ≈ 325 V
2. From 12 = 325 × (1 / 10) × D / (1 − D): 120 (1 − D) = 325 D, so D = 120 / 445 ≈ 0.27
3. Vor = 10 × (12 + 0.5) = 125 V
4. At 265 V AC: Vdc(max) = 265 × √2 ≈ 375 V
5. Vds ≈ 375 + 125 = 500 V before the leakage spike

**Answer:** about 325 V DC bus, D ≈ 0.27 and about 500 V across the MOSFET before ringing. A 650 V or 700 V MOSFET with a clamp circuit is a sensible choice.

</Callout>
