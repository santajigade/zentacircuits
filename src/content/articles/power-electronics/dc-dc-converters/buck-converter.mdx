## What is a buck converter?

A buck converter, also called a step-down converter, takes a DC input and delivers a lower, regulated DC output. You will find one in phone chargers, laptop motherboards and almost every board that powers a 3.3 V microcontroller from a 12 V or [24 V industrial rail](https://instrumentationblog.in/24-volts-in-plc-systems/).

A linear regulator drops the extra voltage across a transistor and turns it into heat. Drop 24 V to 12 V at 2 A that way and you burn 24 W, as much as the load itself. A buck converter avoids most of that loss because its switch is either fully on or fully off.

The power stage needs only four parts: a switch (usually a MOSFET), a diode or a second MOSFET, an inductor and an output capacitor. A controller sets how long the switch stays on in each cycle.

## How a buck converter works

### Switch on (0 to D × T)

When the MOSFET turns on, the input connects to the inductor. The voltage across the inductor is Vin minus Vout, which is positive, so inductor current rises in a straight line.

The diode is reverse biased and carries no current. The inductor stores energy in its magnetic field while it also feeds the load.

### Switch off (D × T to T)

When the MOSFET turns off, the inductor current cannot stop instantly. It pulls the switch node below ground until the diode starts to conduct.

The inductor now sees minus Vout across it, so its current ramps down. Its stored energy flows through the diode into the capacitor and load.

Repeat this 100,000 times a second or more and the inductor current becomes a triangle riding on the load current. The capacitor absorbs the AC part, and the load sees almost flat DC.

## Duty cycle and output voltage

In steady state, inductor current ends each cycle where it started. So the average voltage across the inductor must be zero. Engineers call this volt-second balance.

$$
(V_{in} - V_{out})\,D\,T = V_{out}\,(1 - D)\,T \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_{out} = D \, V_{in}
$$

A 24 V input at 50% [duty cycle](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/pwm-duty-cycle/) therefore gives 12 V. In a real circuit, switch resistance and the diode drop pull the output slightly lower, and the feedback loop raises D a little to correct it.

## Inductor current ripple

During the on time, current rises by a fixed amount and falls by the same amount during the off time:

$$
\Delta I_L = \frac{(V_{in} - V_{out})\,D}{L\,f_s}
$$

A common starting point is ripple of 20% to 40% of full load current. More ripple allows a smaller inductor, but it raises peak current and output ripple.

The MOSFET and inductor must carry the peak current, IL(peak) = Iout + ΔIL / 2. Choose an inductor whose saturation current sits comfortably above this value. For a quick first pass, the [buck converter calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/buck-converter/) gives L, ripple and peak current from your specs.

## Output voltage ripple

The capacitor carries the AC part of the inductor current. With an ideal capacitor, the peak to peak ripple is:

$$
\Delta V_{out} = \frac{\Delta I_L}{8\,f_s\,C}
$$

Real capacitors add ESR, which contributes roughly ΔIL × ESR. With electrolytic capacitors this term often dominates, which is why low ESR ceramic and polymer parts are common in modern designs.

## CCM and DCM

In continuous conduction mode (CCM), inductor current never falls to zero, and every formula above applies.

At light load, the valley of the triangle can touch zero. The converter then enters discontinuous conduction mode (DCM), and the output rises above D × Vin unless the controller cuts D.

The boundary inductance is:

$$
L_{crit} = \frac{(1 - D)\,R}{2\,f_s}
$$

If L is above Lcrit at your lightest load, the converter stays in CCM.

## Losses and efficiency

Four losses matter most. The MOSFET loses about Iout² × RDS(on) × D while on, plus a small switching loss at every edge; a [MOSFET power loss calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/mosfet-power-loss/) estimates both. The diode loses Vf × Iout × (1 − D). The inductor loses Iout² × DCR in its winding.

At low output voltage the diode loss dominates, because Vf is a large fraction of Vout. A synchronous buck replaces the diode with a second MOSFET and removes most of that loss, which is why most modern low voltage regulators are synchronous.

## Buck converter vs linear regulator

Both parts turn a higher DC voltage into a lower one, but they do it in very different ways.

| Feature | Linear regulator | Buck converter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How it drops voltage | Burns the difference as heat | Switches and filters the input |
| Efficiency at 24 V to 12 V | About 50% | Often above 90% |
| Output noise | Very low | Switching ripple, needs filtering |
| Parts count | Very low | Inductor, capacitor, switch, controller |
| Best use | Small currents, noise sensitive circuits | Larger currents, battery and rail conversion |

A linear regulator's best case efficiency is simply Vout / Vin, so 12 V from 24 V can never exceed 50%. That is why designers pick a buck converter whenever the current is more than a few hundred milliamps or the voltage drop is large.

## Where buck converters are used

Buck converters power processors and memory on computer motherboards, charge phone batteries from USB, and feed 5 V and 3.3 V logic from 12 V or 24 V supplies in industrial panels. Solar charge controllers use them to step a high panel voltage down to battery voltage. LED drivers use them in current mode, regulating the inductor current instead of the output voltage.

## Key formulas

| Quantity | Formula (CCM) | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Output voltage | Vout = D × Vin | V |
| Inductor ripple | ΔIL = (Vin − Vout) × D / (L × fs) | A |
| Peak current | IL(peak) = Iout + ΔIL / 2 | A |
| Output ripple | ΔVout = ΔIL / (8 × fs × C) | V |
| CCM boundary | Lcrit = (1 − D) × R / (2 × fs) | H |
| Switch average current | IQ = D × Iout | A |
| Diode average current | ID = (1 − D) × Iout | A |

## Worked example

<div class="box">

**Given:** Vin = 24 V, Vout = 12 V, Iout = 2 A, fs = 100 kHz, L = 47 µH, C = 100 µF

**Find:** D, ΔIL, peak current, output ripple, and whether the converter is in CCM

**Solution:**

1. D = 12 / 24 = 0.50
2. ΔIL = (24 − 12) × 0.5 / (47 µH × 100 kHz) = 6 / 4.7 = 1.28 A
3. IL(peak) = 2 + 0.64 = 2.64 A, valley = 1.36 A
4. ΔVout = 1.28 / (8 × 100 kHz × 100 µF) = 16 mV
5. R = 12 / 2 = 6 Ω, Lcrit = 0.5 × 6 / (2 × 100 kHz) = 15 µH

**Answer:** D = 0.50, ΔIL = 1.28 A (64% of Iout), peak 2.64 A, ripple 16 mV. L = 47 µH is above 15 µH, so the converter runs in CCM.

Note: 64% ripple is above the usual 20% to 40% target. A 100 µH inductor would bring it near 30%.

</div>

## Quick check

An ideal buck converter has Vin = 20 V and D = 0.25. What is Vout? The answer is 5 V, because Vout = D × Vin = 0.25 × 20 V.

If you double the switching frequency while keeping L the same, the inductor ripple halves, because fs sits in the denominator of the ripple formula. The output ripple drops to a quarter, since it depends on both ΔIL and fs.

## Choosing parts step by step

1. **Set the duty cycle** from the input and output voltages, using the lowest and highest input you expect.
2. **Pick the inductor** for 20% to 40% ripple at the highest input voltage, where ripple is largest.
3. **Check the peak current** and choose an inductor and MOSFET rated above it with margin.
4. **Pick the output capacitor** for the ripple you can accept, including its ESR.
5. **Check light load** against Lcrit if your controller must stay in CCM.
6. **Estimate losses** and confirm the MOSFET and diode stay within their temperature limits.

## Common design mistakes

- Picking an inductor by inductance alone and ignoring its saturation current.
- Long switch node traces that ring and radiate [electromagnetic interference](https://instrumentationblog.in/what-is-electromagnetic-interference-emi/).
- Leaving ESR out of the ripple calculation.
- Placing the input capacitor far from the MOSFET. The input loop carries sharp pulsed current and must stay small.
