## What is a Zener diode?

A Zener diode is a silicon PN junction diode designed to operate in reverse breakdown. An ordinary rectifier diode must never reach breakdown in normal use. A Zener is made for it: its doping is set so that breakdown happens at a precise, repeatable voltage called the Zener voltage, Vz.

Forward biased, a Zener behaves like any silicon diode and conducts at about 0.7 V. Reverse biased below Vz, it passes only a tiny leakage current. Once the reverse voltage reaches Vz, it conducts, and the voltage across it stays nearly constant while the current changes over a wide range. That one property makes it a simple voltage regulator and reference.

Zeners are available from about 2.4 V to over 200 V, in standard steps such as 3.3, 5.1, 6.2, 9.1, 12 and 15 V, with power ratings from 0.25 W to 5 W and more.

## How breakdown works

Two physical effects cause reverse breakdown, and which one dominates depends on doping.

**Zener effect.** In heavily doped junctions the depletion layer is very thin, so even a few volts create an intense electric field. The field pulls electrons directly out of their covalent bonds by quantum tunnelling. This dominates below about 5 V.

**Avalanche effect.** In lightly doped junctions the depletion layer is wider. Free carriers accelerate across it and gain enough energy to knock out more electrons, which knock out more again. This dominates above about 5 V.

Both are called Zener diodes in practice, and both are fully reversible as long as the power limit is respected. The two effects have opposite temperature coefficients, which is why parts near 5.6 V have the smallest drift with temperature.

The useful region sits between two currents. Below the knee current Izk the curve rounds off and the voltage is not well defined. Above the maximum current Izm the diode overheats. Between them, the voltage changes only by the current times the small dynamic resistance rz.

## Key Zener diode ratings

| Rating | Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zener voltage | Vz | Breakdown voltage at the test current, often ±5% tolerance |
| Test current | Izt | Current at which Vz and rz are specified |
| Knee current | Izk | Minimum current for reliable regulation |
| Dynamic resistance | rz | Slope ΔVz / ΔIz; lower is better |
| Power rating | Pz | Maximum dissipation, Pz = Vz × Iz |
| Maximum current | Izm | Pz / Vz |
| Temperature coefficient | | Change of Vz with temperature, in %/°C or mV/°C |

For example, the 1N4739A from the common 1 W series is a 9.1 V Zener specified at 28 mA with a dynamic resistance of about 5 Ω.

## Zener diode as a voltage regulator

<figure>
  <img src="/images/figures/zener-regulator-circuit.webp" alt="Zener voltage regulator circuit with series resistor Rs, Zener diode Dz in reverse bias and load resistor RL, showing currents Is, Iz and IL" width="1120" height="520" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Figure 2. Zener shunt regulator. The series resistor drops the extra voltage and the Zener absorbs the current the load does not use.</figcaption>
</figure>

The Zener sits in parallel with the load, cathode to the positive side, and a series resistor Rs connects it to an unregulated input. The current through Rs is:

$$
I_s = \frac{V_{in} - V_z}{R_s} = I_z + I_L
$$

If the input rises, Is rises and the Zener takes the extra current while the output barely moves. If the load draws more, the Zener takes less. Regulation holds as long as Iz stays above its minimum.

The series resistor must be small enough to keep the Zener in regulation at the worst case, which is the lowest input and the highest load:

$$
R_s = \frac{V_{in(min)} - V_z}{I_{L(max)} + I_{z(min)}}
$$

Then check the other worst case, the highest input with no load, where the Zener takes all the current:

$$
P_z = V_z \times \frac{V_{in(max)} - V_z}{R_s}
$$

For quick sizing, the [Zener resistor calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/zener-resistor/) applies these formulas for you.

## Worked example

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example">

**Given:** an unregulated input of 13 V to 17 V, a 9.1 V, 1 W Zener with Iz(min) of 5 mA, load current 0 to 40 mA

**Find:** the series resistor, its power rating and the worst case Zener power

**Solution:**

1. Rs = (13 − 9.1) / (0.040 + 0.005) ≈ 86.7 Ω, so choose 82 Ω
2. Highest input current: (17 − 9.1) / 82 ≈ 96 mA
3. With no load the Zener takes all of it: Pz = 9.1 × 0.096 ≈ 0.88 W
4. Resistor power: (17 − 9.1)² / 82 ≈ 0.76 W

**Answer:** an 82 Ω, 2 W resistor. The 1 W Zener survives at 0.88 W but runs hot, so a 5 W part or a lighter minimum load gives a better margin.

</Callout>

## Boosting the current with a transistor

A plain Zener regulator wastes current, because the Zener must carry the full load current whenever the load is removed. Adding an NPN transistor as an emitter follower solves this. The Zener and its resistor now only supply the transistor's base current, which is the load current divided by the transistor gain. The output is about Vz − 0.7 V, and the Zener power drops to a small fraction of its earlier value.

This series pass regulator was the standard design before three terminal regulator ICs arrived, and it is still used where a simple, rugged supply is needed. Inside a 78xx regulator IC, a bandgap reference does the Zener's job and an error amplifier makes the regulation far tighter.

## Choosing a Zener diode

Start with the voltage, then look at tolerance. Common parts are ±5%, so a 5.1 V Zener may give anywhere from 4.85 V to 5.36 V. For a reference you can tighten this by buying ±2% or ±1% parts, or by trimming afterwards.

Next comes the power rating with margin, because a Zener near its limit runs hot and its voltage drifts. Derate to about half the rated power for long life. Finally, consider temperature. Low voltage Zeners drift downward as they warm and high voltage ones drift upward. A part near 5.6 V, or a dedicated reference IC, gives the best stability.

## Testing a Zener diode

A multimeter diode test only checks the forward junction, which reads about 0.7 V like any diode. To check Vz, feed the Zener from a bench supply set above Vz through a resistor that limits the current to around Izt, with the cathode towards the positive. The voltage across it should read close to its rated value.

## Other applications

- **Voltage reference.** A Zener biased at a constant current gives a stable reference for comparators and ADCs.
- **Overvoltage clamp.** A Zener across a supply rail conducts if the rail rises too far, often with a fuse to make a crowbar.
- **Waveform clipping.** Back to back Zeners limit an AC signal to ±(Vz + 0.7 V).
- **Transistor regulator.** A Zener drives the base of an emitter follower to supply far more current, as in many simple supplies.
- **Input protection.** Small Zeners and TVS diodes shield sensor and logic inputs from spikes.
