## What are logic gates?

Logic gates are the basic building blocks of digital electronics. Each gate takes one or more inputs that are either 0 (low) or 1 (high) and produces a single output that follows a fixed rule. Combine a few gates and you get adders, comparators and multiplexers. Combine millions and you get a microprocessor.

The rules come from Boolean algebra, developed by George Boole in the 1850s and linked to switching circuits by Claude Shannon in 1937. Shannon showed that switches in series behave like AND and switches in parallel behave like OR, and that insight still describes every chip made today.

### NOT gate (inverter)

The NOT gate has one input and gives the opposite at its output: 0 becomes 1 and 1 becomes 0. It is written Y = A′ or with a bar over A.

### AND gate

The output is 1 only when all inputs are 1. Think of two switches in series feeding a lamp. It is written Y = A · B.

### OR gate

The output is 1 when at least one input is 1. Think of two switches in parallel. It is written Y = A + B.

### NAND gate

NAND is AND followed by NOT. The output is 0 only when all inputs are 1. It is the most widely used gate in practice and has its own [NAND gate](/digital/nand-gate/) guide.

### NOR gate

NOR is OR followed by NOT. The output is 1 only when all inputs are 0. Like NAND, it is a universal gate.

### XOR gate (exclusive OR)

The output is 1 when the inputs differ. With more than two inputs, XOR gives 1 when an odd number of inputs are 1, which makes it the basis of parity checkers and binary adders.

### XNOR gate (equivalence)

XNOR is XOR followed by NOT. The output is 1 when the inputs are equal, so it works as a one bit comparator.

## From 0 and 1 to real voltages

Inside a circuit, 0 and 1 are voltage ranges. A gate's datasheet guarantees that a high output is at least VOH and a low output at most VOL, and that it will read any input above VIH as 1 and any input below VIL as 0. Voltages between VIL and VIH are undefined.

The gap between what an output guarantees and what an input needs is the noise margin: NMH = VOH − VIH and NML = VIL − VOL. For 74HC logic at 5 V that is roughly 0.9 V at the high side and 1.2 V at the low side, so a signal can pick up that much noise and still be read correctly. 74LS TTL has smaller margins of about 0.7 V and 0.3 V, which is one reason CMOS replaced it.

<Callout type="warning">
Never leave a CMOS input unconnected. A floating input can sit in the undefined region, making the output oscillate and drawing extra supply current. Tie unused inputs to VCC or ground, directly or through a resistor.
</Callout>

## Propagation delay

A gate does not respond instantly. The output changes a short time after the input, called the propagation delay, tpd. When gates are connected in a chain, the delays add up along the longest path, and that total sets how fast the circuit can run.

## Worked example

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example">

**Given:** an alarm must sound (Y = 1) when the system is armed (A = 1) and a door is open (B = 1), or whenever the panic button is pressed (C = 1)

**Find:** the Boolean expression and the gates needed

**Solution:**

1. "Armed and door open" is A · B, an AND gate
2. "Or panic" adds C with an OR gate
3. Y = A · B + C
4. Check: A = 0, B = 1, C = 0 gives Y = 0; A = 1, B = 1, C = 0 gives Y = 1; any input with C = 1 gives Y = 1

**Answer:** Y = A · B + C, built with one 2 input AND gate feeding one 2 input OR gate. To light an alarm LED from the output, size its series resistor with the [LED resistor calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/led-resistor/).

</Callout>

## Where logic gates are used

- **Arithmetic.** XOR and AND gates form half and full adders, the heart of every ALU.
- **Decision logic.** Interlocks, alarms and safety circuits combine sensor signals with AND and OR logic.
- **Data selection.** Multiplexers, decoders and encoders route data inside processors and memory.
- **Error checking.** XOR trees generate and check parity bits.
- **Memory.** Two cross coupled NAND or NOR gates form a latch, the simplest one bit memory.

## Logic gates with switches and relays

Before transistors, engineers built logic with relays and switches, and the idea still helps when you first learn gates. Two switches in series feeding a lamp form an AND gate: the lamp lights only when both are closed. Two switches in parallel form an OR gate. A normally closed relay contact gives a NOT gate, because the lamp goes off when the coil is energised.

This is exactly how [logic gates in PLC ladder logic](https://www.instrumentationblog.in/logic-gates-in-plc-ladder-logic/) are drawn today. Each rung is a chain of contacts in series and parallel that sets an output coil, so an electrician reading a ladder diagram is really reading AND, OR and NOT logic. Understanding gates makes ladder programs, interlocks and safety circuits much easier to follow.

## Combining gates

Real problems rarely need a single gate. Gates are combined so that the output of one feeds the inputs of others, and the whole circuit still follows one Boolean expression. A half adder, for example, uses one XOR gate for the sum and one AND gate for the carry. Boolean algebra and Karnaugh maps help simplify such expressions so the circuit uses fewer gates and fewer levels, which saves cost and delay.

## Reading a logic gate datasheet

A logic IC datasheet lists more than the truth table. Check the supply range first, then the input and output levels (VIH, VIL, VOH, VOL) at your supply voltage. The output current rating tells you how many inputs or what load one output can drive; CMOS inputs draw almost no current, so fan out is limited mainly by speed and capacitance. Propagation delay is given as typical and maximum values at a stated load capacitance, usually 15 or 50 pF, and it rises with larger loads and lower supply voltages. Always design with the maximum delay, not the typical one.
