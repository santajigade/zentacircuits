## What is a full wave rectifier?

A full wave rectifier turns both halves of an AC sine wave into output pulses of the same polarity. Where a [half wave rectifier](/analog/half-wave-rectifier/) throws away the negative half cycle, a full wave rectifier flips it over and uses it. The result is twice the average output, half the ripple period and a much better use of the transformer.

There are two common ways to build one. The centre tap rectifier uses two diodes and a transformer with a centre tapped secondary. The bridge rectifier uses four diodes and an ordinary secondary winding. Both give the same output waveform; they differ in cost, diode voltage stress and voltage drop.

## Centre tap full wave rectifier

  <figcaption>Figure 1. Centre tap full wave rectifier. Each diode works with one half of the secondary winding.</figcaption>
</figure>

The centre tap is the reference point, and each end of the secondary swings in opposite directions. On the positive half cycle the top end is positive, so D1 conducts and D2 is reverse biased. On the negative half cycle the bottom end is positive, so D2 conducts. Current through the load always flows the same way.

The catch is the transformer. A 12 V output needs a 12-0-12 V winding, which is twice the copper, and each half is used only half the time. The off diode also sees the voltage of both halves, so its peak inverse voltage is about 2Vm.

## Bridge rectifier

<figure>
  <img src="/images/figures/bridge-rectifier-circuit.webp" alt="Bridge rectifier circuit with four diodes D1 to D4 in a diamond, transformer secondary across the AC corners and load across the DC corners" width="1240" height="660" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Figure 2. Bridge rectifier. Two diodes conduct in series on each half cycle.</figcaption>
</figure>

In the bridge, the AC winding connects to two corners of a diamond of four diodes and the load connects to the other two. On the positive half cycle, current flows through D2, the load and D4. On the negative half cycle it flows through D1, the load and D3. Either way it passes through the load in the same direction.

Because two diodes conduct at once, the bridge loses about 1.4 V instead of 0.7 V. In return each diode only has to block about Vm, and the transformer needs a single plain winding with no DC current in it.

## Full wave rectifier formulas

For ideal diodes and a resistive load, the output is |Vm sin ωt|. Its average and RMS values are:

$$
V_{dc} = \frac{2V_m}{\pi} \approx 0.637\,V_m \qquad V_{rms} = \frac{V_m}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

| Quantity | Half wave | Full wave (both types) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Average output Vdc | Vm / π | 2Vm / π |
| RMS output | Vm / 2 | Vm / √2 |
| Form factor | 1.57 | 1.11 |
| Ripple factor | 1.21 | 0.48 |
| Rectification efficiency | 40.6% | 81.2% |
| Ripple frequency | f | 2f |

| Feature | Centre tap | Bridge |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diodes | 2 | 4 |
| Diodes conducting at once | 1 | 2 |
| Voltage drop | About 0.7 V | About 1.4 V |
| Diode PIV | About 2Vm | About Vm |
| Transformer | Centre tapped, twice the winding | Single winding |
| Transformer utilisation factor | 0.693 | 0.812 |

<Callout type="insight">
The bridge trades two extra cheap diodes for a simpler transformer and half the diode voltage stress. That is why almost every mains supply, from phone chargers to industrial DIN rail units, uses a bridge.
</Callout>

## Capacitor filter for a full wave rectifier

A reservoir capacitor across the load charges to the peak on every half cycle and supplies the load between peaks. Because it is recharged twice per cycle, the ripple is half that of a half wave circuit:

$$
\Delta V \approx \frac{I}{2fC}
$$

Rearranged, the capacitor for a target ripple is C ≈ I / (2f × ΔV). The DC output is roughly the peak minus half the ripple. The diode current still flows in short pulses near each peak, so the peak and RMS currents in the diodes and capacitor are several times the load current. Check them against the diode's repetitive peak current and the capacitor's ripple current rating.

## Worked example

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example">

**Given:** a bridge rectifier from a 12 V RMS, 50 Hz secondary, silicon diodes with 0.7 V drop, 500 mA load, 2200 µF capacitor

**Find:** peak output, ripple, DC output and diode PIV

**Solution:**

1. Vm = 12 × √2 ≈ 16.97 V
2. Peak output: 16.97 − 2 × 0.7 ≈ 15.57 V
3. Ripple: ΔV ≈ 0.5 / (2 × 50 × 0.0022) ≈ 2.27 V peak to peak
4. DC output: 15.57 − 2.27 / 2 ≈ 14.4 V
5. PIV per diode: about Vm ≈ 17 V

**Answer:** about 14.4 V DC with 2.3 V of ripple. A 1N4001 (50 V, 1 A) or a 1 A bridge module is suitable, though a 2 A part runs cooler because of the high pulse currents.

</Callout>

## Choosing between centre tap and bridge

Choose the bridge for almost any general purpose supply. It works with a standard single winding, the diodes only need about Vm of reverse rating, and the transformer carries no DC.

Choose the centre tap when the output voltage is low and the current is high, for example a 5 V, 20 A supply. Here one diode drop instead of two saves real power and heat. Low voltage SMPS outputs often use a centre tapped secondary with Schottky diodes or synchronous MOSFETs for this reason. You can read how those stages fit together in the [switched mode power supply](/power-electronics/switched-mode-power-supply/) guide.

## Inrush current at switch on

At the moment of switch on, the reservoir capacitor is empty and behaves almost like a short circuit. If power is applied near the peak of the mains wave, the only things limiting the current are the winding resistance, the diode resistance and the capacitor ESR. The first charging pulse can easily reach ten to fifty times the normal load current.

This surge is why rectifier datasheets list a non repetitive peak forward surge current, IFSM. The 1N4001 series, for example, is rated for 30 A for one half cycle. Large supplies add an NTC thermistor or a resistor with a bypass relay in series with the input to limit the surge. Use the Switch on tab of the simulator above to see the first pulses, then reduce the winding resistance and watch the peak climb.

## Testing a bridge rectifier

With the power off, set a multimeter to diode test. Each of the four diodes should read about 0.5 to 0.7 V in one direction and open circuit in the other. A reading near zero in both directions means a shorted diode; open in both directions means an open diode.

On a bridge module, measure from each AC terminal to the positive and negative terminals. Positive lead on an AC pin and negative lead on the + pin should conduct, as should positive lead on the − pin and negative lead on an AC pin. Under power, an open diode shows up on an oscilloscope as missing pulses: the ripple drops to the supply frequency instead of twice it.

## Applications

- Linear DC power supplies for audio, lab and instrumentation equipment
- The input stage of almost every switched mode power supply
- Battery chargers and DC motor supplies
- Welding machines and electroplating supplies, using high current bridges
- AC signal measurement, where a precision rectifier converts AC to a DC level
