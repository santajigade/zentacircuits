## What is a half wave rectifier?

A half wave rectifier is the simplest circuit that turns alternating current into direct current. It uses a single diode in series with the load. The diode conducts when the input is positive and blocks when it is negative, so only one half of each sine wave reaches the load.

The output is not smooth DC. It is a train of positive half sine pulses with gaps between them. Even so, the current through the load always flows in one direction, and that is what rectification means.

Every DC power supply that runs from the mains starts with a rectifier. Learning the half wave version first makes the [full wave and bridge rectifiers](/analog/full-wave-rectifier/) easy to understand, because they only fill in the missing half cycles.

The transformer steps the mains voltage down to a useful level and isolates the circuit from the mains. The secondary voltage is usually given as an RMS value. Its peak is Vm = √2 × Vrms, so a 12 V RMS winding peaks at about 17 V. To choose the winding for a target voltage, the [transformer turns ratio calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electrical/transformer-turns-ratio/) gives the ratio directly.

## How it works

### Positive half cycle

When the top of the secondary is positive, the diode is forward biased once the voltage passes its knee of about 0.7 V for silicon. Current flows through the diode and the load, and the output follows the input minus the diode drop.

### Negative half cycle

When the secondary reverses, the diode is reverse biased and stops conducting. No current flows, so the output is zero. The whole secondary voltage now appears across the diode as reverse voltage.

## Half wave rectifier formulas

For an ideal diode and a resistive load, the output is v = Vm sin ωt for the positive half and zero for the negative half. Averaging over one full cycle gives:

$$
V_{dc} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi} V_m \sin\theta \, d\theta = \frac{V_m}{\pi} \approx 0.318\,V_m
$$

The RMS value is:

$$
V_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi} V_m^2 \sin^2\theta \, d\theta} = \frac{V_m}{2}
$$

| Quantity | Formula | Value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Average (DC) output | Vdc = Vm / π | 0.318 Vm |
| RMS output | Vrms = Vm / 2 | 0.5 Vm |
| Form factor | Vrms / Vdc = π / 2 | 1.57 |
| Ripple factor | √((Vrms / Vdc)² − 1) | 1.21 |
| Rectification efficiency | Pdc / Pac = 4 / π² | 40.6% |
| Peak inverse voltage | Resistive load: Vm, with capacitor: about 2Vm | |
| Ripple frequency | Same as supply | 50 or 60 Hz |
| Transformer utilisation factor | | 0.287 |

A ripple factor of 1.21 means the AC component of the output is larger than the DC component. That is why a half wave rectifier almost always needs a filter before it can power electronics.
</Callout>

## Effect of a real diode

A real silicon diode needs about 0.7 V before it conducts, and it has a small series resistance. The transformer winding has resistance too. All of these lower the output. A good quick estimate is to replace Vm with Vm − 0.7 V. For low voltage circuits this matters a lot: from a 3 V peak, the diode alone takes almost a quarter of the voltage. Schottky diodes with a drop of 0.3 to 0.5 V help in such cases.

## Adding a capacitor filter

Put a large capacitor across the load and the output changes completely. During the short part of each cycle when the input is near its peak, the diode conducts and charges the capacitor to about Vm − 0.7 V. For the rest of the cycle the diode is off, and the capacitor supplies the load while its voltage falls slowly.

With a load current I, supply frequency f and capacitance C, the peak to peak ripple is approximately:

$$
\Delta V \approx \frac{I}{f\,C}
$$

The DC output is then about Vpeak − ΔV / 2. The energy stored in the capacitor, which you can check with the [capacitor energy calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/capacitor-energy/), is what carries the load through each gap.

Two side effects follow. First, the diode now conducts in short, high current pulses, often five to ten times the load current. Second, the diode's reverse voltage doubles to about 2Vm, because the capacitor holds the cathode near +Vm while the anode swings down to −Vm.

## Worked example

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example">

**Given:** a 12 V RMS secondary at 50 Hz, a silicon diode with 0.7 V drop and a 100 mA load

**Find:** the DC output without a filter, and the ripple and DC output with a 1000 µF capacitor

**Solution:**

1. Vm = 12 × √2 ≈ 16.97 V
2. Peak at the load: 16.97 − 0.7 ≈ 16.27 V
3. Without a capacitor: Vdc ≈ 16.27 / π ≈ 5.18 V
4. With 1000 µF: ΔV ≈ 0.1 / (50 × 0.001) = 2 V peak to peak
5. Vdc ≈ 16.27 − 2 / 2 ≈ 15.3 V
6. Diode PIV with the capacitor: about 16.97 + 16.27 ≈ 33 V

**Answer:** about 5.2 V without a filter and about 15.3 V with 2 V of ripple with the capacitor. A 1N4001 (50 V) has enough reverse voltage margin.

</Callout>

The simulator gives slightly lower values because it includes the 0.5 Ω winding resistance and the diode's own resistance.

## Advantages and disadvantages

The half wave rectifier needs only one diode, so it is cheap and easy to build. It works well where the current is tiny, such as in signal and peak detectors.

Its weaknesses are serious for power supplies. Half the input is wasted, the ripple frequency is low so the filter capacitor must be large, and the DC current through the secondary can partly saturate the transformer core. This is why mains supplies use full wave or bridge rectifiers instead.

## Transformer utilisation factor explained

Transformer utilisation factor, or TUF, compares the DC power delivered to the load with the volt-ampere rating the transformer secondary must have. For a half wave rectifier it is only about 0.287, which means a transformer rated at 10 VA can deliver under 3 W of DC.

There are two reasons. The secondary carries current for only half of each cycle, so its RMS current is high compared with the DC current. Worse, that current always flows in one direction, so the core sees a steady DC magnetising force. This pushes the core towards saturation, raises the magnetising current and heats the transformer. Designers therefore either oversize the transformer or, far more often, use a full wave circuit, where the secondary current is pure AC.

## Testing a half wave rectifier on the bench

A digital multimeter and an oscilloscope tell you almost everything. With no capacitor fitted, measure the output on the DC range: it should read close to 0.318 times the peak minus the diode drop. On the AC range the meter shows the ripple content, which for this circuit is larger than the DC reading.

On an oscilloscope, connect the ground clip to the negative output and look at the load. You should see clean half sine pulses at the mains frequency. Flat tops mean the diode or winding is overloaded; no output at all usually means an open diode or blown fuse. With the capacitor fitted, the ripple should look like a sawtooth that repeats every 20 ms on 50 Hz mains. If it repeats every 10 ms, the circuit is actually a full wave design.

## Applications

- Signal demodulation in AM radio receivers
- Peak detectors and voltage measurement circuits
- Low current auxiliary supplies inside appliances
- Voltage multiplier stages, which are built from half wave sections
- Input protection and polarity protection with a single series diode
