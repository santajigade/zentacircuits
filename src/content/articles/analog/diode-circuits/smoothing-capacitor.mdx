## What is a smoothing capacitor?

A smoothing capacitor is a large capacitor connected across the output of a rectifier. On its own, a [full wave rectifier](/analog/full-wave-rectifier/) delivers a train of half sine pulses that drop to zero twice every cycle. That is DC in the sense that it never reverses, but no electronic circuit can run from it. The smoothing capacitor fills in the gaps and turns those pulses into a steady voltage with only a small ripple.

The same part goes by several names: reservoir capacitor, filter capacitor or bulk capacitor. Every linear power supply and every mains powered switched mode supply has one, and choosing it well decides how clean the output is, how hot the diodes run and how long the supply lasts.

## How a smoothing capacitor works

Each cycle has two phases.

**Charging.** As the rectified voltage rises near its peak, it climbs above the capacitor voltage. The diodes conduct and current flows both into the load and into the capacitor, topping it up to the peak voltage minus the diode drops.

**Discharging.** Once the rectified voltage falls past its peak, it drops below the capacitor voltage. The diodes turn off and the capacitor alone feeds the load, so its voltage sags slowly until the next pulse arrives.

The result is a DC output with a sawtooth shaped ripple on top. The bigger the capacitor, or the lighter the load, the slower it sags and the smaller the ripple.

## Ripple voltage formula

If we assume the capacitor supplies a constant load current I for the whole gap between pulses, its voltage falls by ΔV = I × t / C. The gap is about one ripple period, which is 1 / (2f) for a full wave or bridge rectifier and 1 / f for a half wave rectifier:

$$
\Delta V \approx \frac{I}{2fC} \;\text{(full wave)} \qquad \Delta V \approx \frac{I}{fC} \;\text{(half wave)}
$$

The DC output is roughly the peak minus half the ripple: Vdc ≈ Vpeak − ΔV / 2.

<Callout type="insight">
The formula overestimates the ripple, by around 20% in this example. The capacitor does not discharge for the whole half cycle, because part of each cycle is spent recharging it. That makes I / (2fC) a safe, conservative design rule.
</Callout>

## Sizing a smoothing capacitor

Rearrange the ripple formula to find the capacitance for a chosen ripple:

$$
C \approx \frac{I}{2f\,\Delta V}
$$

Then pick the next standard value up, and allow for tolerance. Aluminium electrolytics are often −20% / +20%, and they lose capacitance as they age, so a little extra margin is wise. The [capacitor energy calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/capacitor-energy/) shows how much energy the chosen value stores at the peak voltage.

## The hidden cost of a bigger capacitor

A larger capacitor gives less ripple, but it does not come free. Because the ripple is smaller, the rectified voltage only rises above the capacitor voltage for a short moment near each peak. The diodes must deliver a whole half cycle of charge in that narrow window, so their current flows in short, tall pulses.

| Capacitor | V dc | Ripple p-p | Conduction per pulse | Peak diode current | Capacitor ripple current |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 µF | 12.1 V | 7.3 V | 73° | 0.50 A | 0.18 A |
| 470 µF | 14.3 V | 2.4 V | 41° | 1.03 A | 0.30 A |
| 1000 µF | 14.7 V | 1.2 V | 35° | 1.15 A | 0.34 A |
| 2200 µF | 14.8 V | 0.55 V | 34° | 1.19 A | 0.34 A |

These values come from the simulator for a bridge rectifier from 12 V RMS into 100 Ω, a load current of about 0.15 A. Going from 470 µF to 2200 µF cuts the ripple by more than four times, while the peak diode current is already about eight times the load current. The winding resistance limits how narrow and tall the pulses can get, which is why the last row barely changes.

## Choosing the right capacitor

**Voltage rating.** Find the highest peak voltage the capacitor can see: maximum mains voltage, no load, with the transformer's light load voltage rise. Choose a rating at least 1.25 to 1.5 times that value. A 12 V RMS winding peaks near 17 V, and closer to 19 V at high mains and no load, so a 25 V capacitor is the minimum and 35 V gives comfortable margin.

**Ripple current rating.** The capacitor current is not small. It flows in and out every cycle, heating the capacitor through its equivalent series resistance (ESR). Compare the RMS ripple current from the simulator with the datasheet rating at 100 or 120 Hz, corrected for temperature.

**Temperature and life.** Electrolytic life roughly doubles for every 10 °C drop in temperature. A 105 °C rated part, mounted away from heat sinks, lasts far longer than an 85 °C part. The [capacitor lifetime calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/capacitor-lifetime/) estimates the life at your operating temperature.

**Bleeder resistor.** In high voltage supplies, add a resistor across the capacitor so it discharges within a few seconds after power off.

## Worked example

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example">

**Given:** a bridge rectifier from a 12 V RMS, 50 Hz winding, load current about 0.5 A, maximum ripple 1.5 V peak to peak

**Find:** the smoothing capacitor and its ratings

**Solution:**

1. C ≈ I / (2f × ΔV) = 0.5 / (2 × 50 × 1.5) ≈ 3,330 µF
2. Choose a 3,300 µF capacitor, or 4,700 µF to allow for −20% tolerance and ageing
3. Peak voltage at no load and high mains: about 19 V, so choose a 35 V rating
4. In the simulator, even 2,200 µF gives about 1.5 V of ripple at this load, confirming that the formula is conservative
5. Ripple current is about 0.85 A RMS, so check the datasheet rating at 100 Hz

**Answer:** a 3,300 µF or 4,700 µF, 35 V, 105 °C electrolytic rated for at least 1 A of ripple current.

</Callout>

## Inrush current at switch on

An empty capacitor looks like a short circuit at the moment of switch on. If power arrives near the peak of the mains wave, only the winding, diode and capacitor resistances limit the first charging pulse, which can reach tens or hundreds of amperes in a large supply. The larger the capacitor, the longer this surge lasts. Small supplies rely on the transformer's resistance; larger ones add an NTC thermistor or a resistor that is bypassed by a relay once the capacitor is charged. Always check that the rectifier's surge current rating covers the first pulse.

## Going further: CRC and LC filters

When a single capacitor cannot reach the ripple you need, add a second stage. A CRC (pi) filter places a resistor and a second capacitor after the first, trading a small voltage drop for much lower ripple. An LC filter uses an inductor instead of the resistor, losing very little voltage. The [LC filter calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/lc-filter/) helps size it. For sensitive circuits the usual answer is a regulator after the smoothing capacitor, such as a [Zener regulator](/components/zener-diode/) for small loads or a linear regulator IC.
