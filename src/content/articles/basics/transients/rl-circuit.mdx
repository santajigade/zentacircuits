## What is an RL circuit?

An RL circuit is a resistor (R) and an inductor (L) connected to a voltage source. It is the magnetic twin of the [RC circuit](/basics/rc-circuit/). A capacitor resists changes in voltage; an inductor resists changes in current. Put an inductor in series with a resistor and the current can no longer jump. It rises and falls along an exponential curve with a predictable time constant.

RL behaviour is everywhere in practical electronics. Every relay coil, solenoid, motor winding and transformer primary is an inductor with some resistance. Understanding the RL circuit explains why relays pull in a little late, why transistors driving coils need a diode, and why power converters use inductors to smooth current.

## The time constant τ = L / R

The time constant of an RL circuit is the inductance divided by the resistance:

$$
\tau = \frac{L}{R}
$$

With L = 10 mH and R = 100 Ω, τ = 100 µs. A 0.5 H relay coil with 120 Ω of winding resistance has τ ≈ 4.2 ms. After each τ the current covers 63.2% of the change still remaining, the same percentages as in an RC circuit: 63.2% after 1τ, 86.5% after 2τ, 95.0% after 3τ and 99.3% after 5τ. The [RL time constant calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/rl-time-constant/) works it out for any pair of values.

Notice one important difference from RC. Increasing R makes an RL circuit faster, because τ = L / R gets smaller. The current then settles to a lower final value, V / R.

## Current rise after a step

When the switch closes, the current starts at zero because an inductor's current cannot change instantly. At that instant the whole supply voltage appears across the inductor. As the current grows, more voltage drops across R and less is left across L, so the rate of rise slows:

$$
i(t) = \frac{V}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right) \qquad v_L(t) = V\,e^{-t/\tau}
$$

## Current decay and inductive kickback

If the source is replaced by a short, the current decays along the mirror curve:

$$
i(t) = I_0\,e^{-t/\tau}
$$

The danger comes when the circuit is simply opened. The inductor stores energy E = ½ L I² in its magnetic field and will try to keep the current flowing. With nowhere to go, the current falls almost instantly, and the induced voltage v = L di/dt becomes enormous. A small relay coil switched off by a transistor can produce hundreds of volts for a fraction of a microsecond, enough to break down the transistor or arc across a switch contact. The [inductor energy calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/inductor-energy/) shows how much energy has to go somewhere.

<Callout type="warning">
Always give an inductive load a path for its current when it is switched off. A flyback diode across a DC relay coil, a TVS diode across a solenoid driver, or an RC snubber across an AC contact keeps the spike to a safe level.
</Callout>

With a flyback diode, the coil current circulates through the diode and the coil resistance after turn off, decaying with the time constant L / R. The voltage is clamped to one diode drop above the supply. The trade off is a slower release, because the current takes longer to decay. Where fast release matters, a Zener in series with the diode lets the current fall faster while still limiting the voltage.

## Square wave response

Drive an RL circuit with a square wave and the current follows the same rounded edges you see in an RC circuit. The resistor voltage, which is proportional to current, rises and falls exponentially. The inductor voltage shows sharp spikes at each edge that decay with τ. When τ is much longer than the half period, the current barely changes and settles to a triangle around its average, which is exactly how the inductor in a [buck converter](/power-electronics/buck-converter/) smooths a switched voltage into steady current.

## RL filter: low pass and high pass

In AC terms an inductor has a reactance XL = 2πfL that rises with frequency. In a series RL circuit the source voltage splits between R and XL:

- **Output across R (low pass).** At low frequency XL is small, so most of the voltage appears across R. At high frequency the inductor blocks the current and the output falls.
- **Output across L (high pass).** The opposite: low frequencies are shorted by L and high frequencies appear across it.

The cutoff frequency, where XL equals R, is:

$$
f_c = \frac{R}{2\pi L}
$$

At fc the output is 3 dB down with a 45° phase shift, and beyond it a first order RL filter rolls off at 20 dB per decade. The [reactance calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/reactance/) gives XL at any frequency. In signal circuits RC filters are more common because capacitors are cheaper and closer to ideal; RL filters appear where current is high, such as power line filters and converter outputs.

## Worked example

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example">

**Given:** a 12 V relay coil with R = 120 Ω and L = 0.5 H, which pulls in at 70 mA

**Find:** the final current, the time constant, the pull in delay and the stored energy

**Solution:**

1. Final current: 12 / 120 = 0.1 A
2. τ = 0.5 / 120 ≈ 4.17 ms
3. 70 mA is 70% of the final current, so t = −4.17 × ln(1 − 0.7) ≈ 5.0 ms
4. Stored energy: ½ × 0.5 × 0.1² = 2.5 mJ

**Answer:** the coil current settles at 100 mA, the relay pulls in about 5 ms after the voltage is applied, and 2.5 mJ must be dissipated by a flyback diode when it is switched off.

</Callout>

## RC circuit vs RL circuit

| Feature | RC circuit | RL circuit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time constant | τ = RC | τ = L / R |
| Quantity that cannot jump | Capacitor voltage | Inductor current |
| Effect of larger R | Slower | Faster |
| Stored energy | ½ C V² | ½ L I² |
| Low pass output | Across C | Across R |
| Typical use | Timing, signal filters | Relays, motors, power filters |

## Where RL circuits are used

- **Relay and solenoid drivers.** The coil is an RL load; its time constant sets the pull in delay and its stored energy sets the flyback diode rating.
- **Motor windings.** Current in a DC motor rises with the winding's L / R, which limits how fast torque can change.
- **Switching converters.** The inductor in buck and boost converters relies on RL behaviour to smooth current.
- **Inrush and current limiting.** Series inductors and chokes limit how quickly current can rise.
- **EMI filters.** Common mode chokes and series inductors block high frequency noise on power lines.

## Measuring τ on a real coil

Measuring L / R directly is easy with a function generator and an oscilloscope. Put a small known resistor in series with the coil and feed a slow square wave. The voltage across the resistor is proportional to the coil current. Measure the time it takes to reach 63.2% of its final value; that is τ, with the series resistor and the generator's output resistance added to the coil's own resistance. Multiply τ by the total resistance to estimate the inductance. Remember that inductance of an iron cored coil changes with current, so test near the real operating current.
