## What is an RC circuit?

An RC circuit is a resistor (R) and a capacitor (C) connected to a voltage source. It is one of the most useful circuits in electronics because it introduces time. A resistor responds to a voltage instantly; a capacitor stores charge, so its voltage can only change gradually. Put the two together and you get a predictable delay that sets the timing of timers, filters, reset circuits and oscillators.

In this guide we look at the series RC circuit in both of its roles: as a transient circuit that charges and discharges after a voltage step, and as a filter that passes some frequencies and blocks others.

## The time constant τ = RC

The product of resistance and capacitance has the unit of seconds and is called the time constant:

$$
\tau = R \times C
$$

A 1 kΩ resistor with a 1 µF capacitor gives τ = 1 ms. A 10 kΩ resistor with 100 µF gives 1 s. The time constant tells you everything about the speed of the circuit: after each τ, the capacitor has covered 63.2% of whatever change was still left. To work out τ for any pair of values, the [RC time constant calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/physics/rc-time-constant/) gives it in one step.

| Time | Charging, % of V | Discharging, % of V0 left |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1τ | 63.2% | 36.8% |
| 2τ | 86.5% | 13.5% |
| 3τ | 95.0% | 5.0% |
| 4τ | 98.2% | 1.8% |
| 5τ | 99.3% | 0.7% |

## Charging an RC circuit

When the switch closes at t = 0 on an empty capacitor, the capacitor voltage cannot jump. It starts at zero, so the full source voltage V appears across the resistor and the starting current is V / R. As the capacitor charges, its voltage rises, the voltage across R falls and the current falls with it. That self slowing is what makes the curve exponential:

$$
v_C(t) = V\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right) \qquad i(t) = \frac{V}{R}\,e^{-t/RC}
$$

## Discharging an RC circuit

If a capacitor charged to V0 is connected across a resistor, it discharges along the mirror image curve:

$$
v_C(t) = V_0\,e^{-t/RC}
$$

To find the time to reach a certain voltage, rearrange the equation with a natural log. For charging, t = −RC × ln(1 − vC / V). For discharging, t = RC × ln(V0 / vC). These two equations are the basis of every RC timer, including the 555 timer, which compares the capacitor voltage with thresholds at one third and two thirds of the supply.

<Callout type="insight">
The energy needed to charge a capacitor through a resistor is split exactly in half: half is stored in the capacitor as ½CV² and half is lost as heat in the resistor, whatever the value of R. You can check the stored part with the [capacitor energy calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/capacitor-energy/).
</Callout>

## Square wave response

Feed an RC circuit with a square wave and the result depends on how τ compares with the square wave's half period.

- **τ much shorter than the half period:** the capacitor fully charges and discharges on every edge. The capacitor voltage is a square wave with rounded corners, and the resistor voltage shows sharp spikes on each edge. Taken across R, this is a differentiator.
- **τ about equal to the half period:** the capacitor never fully charges, giving the familiar shark fin shape.
- **τ much longer than the half period:** the capacitor voltage barely moves and becomes a small triangle wave around the average. Taken across C, this is an integrator.

Try all three in the simulator by changing the source frequency with Square selected.

## RC filter: low pass and high pass

In AC terms a capacitor has a reactance XC = 1 / (2πfC) that falls as frequency rises. In a series RC circuit, the input voltage divides between R and XC:

- **Output across C (low pass).** At low frequency XC is large, so most of the voltage appears across C. At high frequency C acts like a short and the output falls.
- **Output across R (high pass).** The opposite: low frequencies are blocked by C and high frequencies pass.

Both filters have the same cutoff frequency, where XC equals R:

$$
f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}
$$

  <img src="/images/figures/rc-low-pass-filter-bode-plot.webp" alt="Bode plot of an RC low pass filter from the simulator, showing a flat response, minus 3 dB at the 159 Hz cutoff and a minus 20 dB per decade roll off" width="1440" height="780" loading="lazy" />

At fc the output is 70.7% of the input and shifted by 45°. Above fc a first order RC filter rolls off at 20 dB per decade, which is 6 dB per octave. For quick filter sizing, the [RC filter calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/rc-filter/) works out fc or the component values, and the [reactance calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/reactance/) shows XC at any frequency.

## Worked example

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example">

**Given:** a 12 V supply charges a 100 µF capacitor through 10 kΩ from zero

**Find:** the time constant, the starting current and the time to reach 9 V

**Solution:**

1. τ = 10,000 × 100 × 10⁻⁶ = 1 s
2. Starting current: 12 / 10,000 = 1.2 mA
3. 9 V is 75% of 12 V, so t = −1 × ln(1 − 0.75) = ln 4 ≈ 1.39 s
4. Nearly full charge: 5τ = 5 s

**Answer:** τ = 1 s, the current starts at 1.2 mA, and the capacitor reaches 9 V after about 1.39 s.

</Callout>

## Where RC circuits are used

- **Timing and delay.** Power on reset circuits, 555 timers and delayed relay drivers.
- **Switch debouncing.** An RC filter smooths the bounces of a mechanical contact before a logic input.
- **Filtering.** Anti aliasing before an ADC, removing noise from sensor signals and smoothing PWM into an analog voltage.
- **Coupling and decoupling.** A series capacitor blocks DC between amplifier stages; a capacitor from supply to ground removes high frequency noise.
- **Snubbers.** An RC network across a switch or relay contact absorbs voltage spikes.

## Measuring τ on a real circuit

Drive the circuit with a square wave from a function generator, slow enough that the capacitor fully charges on each half cycle. On the oscilloscope, measure from the rising edge to the point where the capacitor voltage reaches 63.2% of its full swing. That interval is τ. Most scopes can place cursors at these levels; the simulator above does the same with C1 and C2.

To measure fc, use a sine wave and sweep its frequency until the output amplitude is 70.7% of the input. Remember that the scope probe and the source resistance both load the circuit, so for small capacitors and large resistors the measured values can differ from theory.

## RC circuit vs RL circuit

An inductor with a resistor behaves in a similar way, but with the roles of voltage and current swapped. In an RL circuit the current cannot change instantly, so it rises exponentially with a time constant τ = L / R, while the inductor voltage starts at the full supply and decays. RL circuits appear in relay coils, motor windings and current limiting in power converters. When the current through an inductor is suddenly interrupted, it produces a large voltage spike, which is why relay coils need a flyback diode. RC circuits are more common for timing because capacitors are cheaper, smaller and closer to ideal than inductors at low frequencies. The [RL time constant calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/rl-time-constant/) covers the inductor version.
