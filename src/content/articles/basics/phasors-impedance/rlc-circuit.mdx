## What is an RLC circuit?

An RLC circuit contains a resistor, an inductor and a capacitor. In the series form they share one loop and one current. The inductor stores energy in its magnetic field and the capacitor stores it in its electric field, and energy can swing back and forth between them. That exchange gives the circuit a natural frequency, and the resistor decides how quickly the swing dies away.

This makes the RLC circuit the electrical version of a mass on a spring with friction. It explains radio tuning, filters, oscillators and the ringing you see on fast switching edges. If you have not met time constants yet, read the [RC circuit](/basics/rc-circuit/) guide first, because the RLC circuit builds on it.

## Impedance of a series RLC circuit

With a sine wave source, the inductor's reactance XL = 2πfL rises with frequency and the capacitor's reactance XC = 1 / (2πfC) falls. They act in opposite directions, so the net reactance is their difference:

$$
Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2} \qquad \phi = \tan^{-1}\frac{X_L - X_C}{R}
$$

Below resonance XC wins and the circuit is capacitive, with current leading the voltage. Above resonance XL wins and the circuit is inductive, with current lagging. The [reactance calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/reactance/) gives XL and XC at any frequency.

## Resonant frequency

At one frequency the two reactances are equal and cancel completely. The impedance falls to R alone, the current is at its maximum and in phase with the source:

$$
f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}
$$

With L = 10 mH and C = 1 µF, f0 = 1 / (2π × 10⁻⁴) ≈ 1.59 kHz. Note that R does not appear: the resistance changes the height and width of the peak, never its position. For quick values, the [resonant frequency calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/resonant-frequency/) solves the formula for any L and C.

## Q factor and bandwidth

The quality factor Q describes how sharp the resonance is:

$$
Q = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} = \frac{X_L}{R}\Big|_{f_0} = \frac{f_0}{BW}
$$

The bandwidth BW is the gap between the two half power frequencies, where the current has fallen to 70.7% of its peak. For a series circuit BW = R / (2πL). A high Q gives a narrow band that picks out one frequency, which is what a radio tuner needs. A low Q gives a broad, gentle response.

## Damping and step response

Apply a sudden DC step instead of a sine wave and the circuit answers with its own natural behaviour. The damping ratio decides what that looks like:

$$
\zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}} = \frac{1}{2Q}
$$

| Damping ratio | Name | Step response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ζ < 1 | Underdamped | Overshoots and rings at fd = f0√(1 − ζ²) while decaying |
| ζ = 1 | Critically damped | Fastest rise with no overshoot |
| ζ > 1 | Overdamped | Slow rise with no overshoot |

The resistance that gives critical damping is R = 2√(L/C). For an underdamped circuit the first overshoot of the capacitor voltage is e^(−πζ / √(1 − ζ²)) of the step, which is 16.3% when ζ = 0.5 and 85% when ζ = 0.05.

## Worked example

<Callout type="example" title="Worked example">

**Given:** a radio tuning circuit with L = 100 µH, C = 100 pF and R = 10 Ω, driven by a 1 V signal

**Find:** f0, Q, bandwidth and the voltage across C at resonance

**Solution:**

1. √(LC) = √(10⁻⁴ × 10⁻¹⁰) = 10⁻⁷, so f0 = 1 / (2π × 10⁻⁷) ≈ 1.59 MHz
2. Q = (1 / 10) × √(10⁻⁴ / 10⁻¹⁰) = (1 / 10) × 1,000 = 100
3. BW = f0 / Q ≈ 15.9 kHz
4. Current at resonance: 1 / 10 = 0.1 A
5. Voltage across C: Q × 1 V = 100 V

**Answer:** the circuit tunes to about 1.59 MHz, in the AM broadcast band, with a 15.9 kHz bandwidth and 100 V across the capacitor at resonance.

</Callout>

## How energy moves inside an RLC circuit

Picture a charged capacitor connected to an inductor through a small resistor. The capacitor pushes current into the inductor, and its voltage falls as its electric field empties. By the time the capacitor voltage reaches zero, all the energy sits in the inductor's magnetic field and the current is at its peak. The inductor now keeps the current flowing and charges the capacitor in the opposite direction. The energy swings back and forth at the natural frequency, just like a pendulum moving between potential and kinetic energy.

Every pass through the resistor turns a little of that energy into heat. With a small R the swing lasts for many cycles, which is the high Q, underdamped case. With a large R the energy is used up before a single swing completes, which is the overdamped case. Q is in fact defined by this: it is 2π times the energy stored divided by the energy lost per cycle.

## Phase across resonance

The phase of the current tells you which side of resonance you are on. Well below f0 the capacitor dominates and the current leads the source voltage by almost 90°. At f0 the phase is exactly zero, so the circuit draws power like a plain resistor. Well above f0 the inductor dominates and the current lags by almost 90°. The phase passes through ±45° at the two half power frequencies. You can see this on the Frequency response tab by choosing V R as the output, since the resistor voltage is in phase with the current.

## Measuring resonance on the bench

Connect a function generator through the series circuit and put an oscilloscope across the resistor, because the resistor voltage is proportional to the current. Sweep the sine frequency and find where the resistor voltage peaks; that is f0. Then find the two frequencies on either side where the voltage drops to 70.7% of the peak, subtract them to get the bandwidth, and divide f0 by it for Q. Expect the measured Q to be lower than the calculated one, because the generator's 50 Ω output resistance and the inductor's winding resistance add to R.

## Series vs parallel resonance

| Feature | Series RLC | Parallel RLC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Impedance at f0 | Minimum, equal to R | Maximum |
| Current from source at f0 | Maximum | Minimum |
| Magnified quantity | Voltage across L and C | Current circulating in L and C |
| Typical use | Selecting a frequency, series filters | Tank circuits in oscillators, notch filters |

## Where RLC circuits are used

- **Radio tuning.** A variable capacitor moves f0 to select one station.
- **Filters.** Band pass and band stop filters and LC output filters in power converters. The [LC filter calculator](https://zentacalculator.com/electronics/lc-filter/) helps size them.
- **Oscillators.** LC oscillators such as Colpitts and Hartley use the resonance to set the frequency.
- **Wireless power and induction heating.** Both run at resonance to transfer large currents efficiently.
- **Resonant converters.** LLC power supplies use series resonance to switch with very low losses.

## Unwanted resonance

Every real circuit has some stray inductance and capacitance, so RLC behaviour appears where nobody designed it. The ringing on a MOSFET drain, overshoot on long cable runs and noise on a power rail all come from parasitic resonance. Engineers tame it with damping: an RC snubber, a series gate resistor or a lossy ferrite bead adds the R that lowers Q. The same damping ratio formula tells you how much resistance is enough.
